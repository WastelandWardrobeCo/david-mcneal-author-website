I’m Just Amy

Read Chapter One

Chapter One

Hair was always the hardest part.

Not the bones.

Bones were geometry. Load distribution, mineral density, microscopic channels for blood vessels and marrow. Complicated, certainly, but obedient. Once the printer understood the architecture, it could reproduce a femur as easily as a length of pipe.

The heart was plumbing and rhythm. Humanity had solved that problem years ago.

Lungs were membranes. Kidneys were filters. Skin was layers, tension, and pigmentation.

Hair refused simplicity.

Millions of individual strands, each emerging from a living follicle at its own angle. Too uniform and it looked artificial. Too random and it looked diseased. A human scalp did not forgive shortcuts. It displayed them.

Dr. Benjamin Carter leaned toward the observation glass and studied the crown of the child’s head.

“Density?”

“Final scan is resolving,” Emily Ortiz said.

She sat at the follicular console with one hand wrapped around a travel mug large enough to qualify as medical equipment and the other moving across the display. At twenty-eight, Emily was the youngest person in the room and regarded coffee as a constitutional right.

The scan finished.

“Ninety-nine point nine nine four percent.”

Ben looked at her.

Emily closed her eyes briefly. “I know.”

“How many?”

“Three hundred and twelve missing follicles.”

“Again.”

“Doctor Carter, there are approximately five million follicles across the human body.”

“Three hundred and twelve are missing.”

“No actual person has every follicle.”

“This one will.”

Emily glanced through the glass.

“Almost.”

“Almost isn’t the assignment.”

A few tired faces turned toward them, then quickly returned to their stations.

Emily set down her coffee.

“Running another pass.”

The printer arm moved above the chamber with a sound barely louder than breath. Beneath it, molecular assembly heads corrected three hundred and twelve errors no unaided eye could have seen.

Ben watched the progress bar.

On the counter beside him sat a bowl of oatmeal he had prepared before dawn. He’d carried it to the console with every intention of eating it.

Then someone had called out a calibration discrepancy.

The oatmeal had never made it past the first bite.

Now the surface had developed a pale skin.

He checked the clock.

9:17 a.m.

Thirteen years, four months, and six days after the project’s first successful printed kidney, they were preparing to activate a complete human brain.

No cameras waited outside.

No politicians stood behind the glass.

No champagne chilled in a conference room.

The people who funded the project had stopped attending finalization cycles after Subject 209. Watching a biologically complete human fail to become a person had apparently lost its novelty.

“Pass complete,” Emily said.

“Density?”

“One hundred percent.”

“Lock it.”

She entered the command.

Inside the chamber, the child floated quietly in clear biogenic fluid.

She wore a seamless white integration garment that covered her torso and upper legs. The material was thin enough for scanners, sensors, and infusion lines to function through it, but fully opaque.

The garment had been Ben’s policy.

Years earlier, an administrator had complained that it served no measurable purpose.

Ben had answered that dignity rarely did.

The argument had ended there.

Now the child’s brown hair drifted around her face. Her eyes remained closed. Her arms rested loosely at her sides.

She looked asleep.

She wasn’t.

Sleep required a mind capable of waking.

Emily came to stand beside him.

“Do you ever get used to it?” she asked.

“To what?”

“The moment when they stop looking manufactured.”

Ben watched the child through the glass.

“No.”

It happened before every activation.

The components disappeared.

There was no printed liver. No fabricated skeleton. No constructed vascular network.

There was only a person-shaped silence waiting for something they had never been able to provide.

Subject 247 had been built at an estimated biological age of nine years and two months.

Adult bodies were easier to monitor and stabilize, but a mature brain was more than completed architecture. It was years of language, sensation, memory, attachment, pain, and repetition.

Printing the finished shape, Ben had argued, might be no more useful than printing a book with every page blank.

So they had made this one younger. Old enough for her organs to survive integration. Young enough, perhaps, for the brain to become something more.

A little under four feet four inches tall. Brown eyes. Brown hair. A scattering of freckles predicted to darken in sunlight.

She had not been designed to be beautiful, brilliant, strong, or exceptional. She had been designed to be healthy.

That alone had proved difficult enough.

At the central station, Dr. Elias Venn cleared his throat.

“We’re ready.”

Ben looked across the room.

People straightened. Coffee cups were pushed aside. Screens changed from manufacturing displays to neurological monitoring.

Fifty-four compounds would be introduced during the final maturation sequence. Each had its own concentration, timing, and delivery rate.

Ben understood every one of them.

What mattered was that the sequence had been performed before.

Again and again.

It had made hearts beat.

Lungs breathe.

Muscles react.

It had never made anyone wake up.

Ben moved to the main console.

“Reservoirs?”

“Full and primed,” Venn said.

“Circulation?”

“Stable.”

“Core temperature?”

“Normal.”

“Neural baseline?”

“Minimal spontaneous activity.”

The room settled into silence.

Not reverence.

Routine.

They had all been here before.

Ben placed his thumb against the authorization pad.

“Begin final integration.”

The system accepted his print.

A column of green indicators illuminated the monitor.

NEURAL MATURATION ACTIVE

The first compounds entered the child’s bloodstream.

For several minutes, nothing changed.

Her heart continued its steady rhythm. Her chest moved gently beneath the white garment. Electrical flickers crossed the brain map and vanished before they formed anything recognizable.

Ben had watched this happen one hundred and thirty-eight times.

The monitors would brighten.

The heart would strengthen.

Fingers might move.

Eyelids might tremble.

Then the activity would settle into meaningless electrical weather.

A body without anyone inside it.

He had stopped expecting more around Subject Eighty.

Hope was exhausting.

“Coherence at eleven percent,” a technician said.

Nobody reacted.

Eleven was ordinary.

The child’s fingers moved once, then relaxed.

“Fourteen percent.”

Emily glanced up.

“Higher than 242,” she said.

“Not by much,” Ben replied.

The project record was nineteen.

Subject 242 had reached eighteen point seven percent before neurological activity collapsed into seizure patterns.

“Sixteen.”

The child’s eyelids trembled.

Ben watched the brain map.

The patterns were still fragmented, still fleeting, but they were beginning to cross regions that had never communicated cleanly in previous trials.

“Seventeen percent.”

Someone behind Ben stopped opening a packet of crackers.

“Eighteen.”

No one spoke.

“Nineteen.”

The technician looked at Ben.

“That matches the record.”

Ben did not answer.

The number changed.

“Twenty-one percent.”

Emily slowly set down her coffee.

Across the room, chairs scraped as people leaned toward their monitors.

“Confirm calibration,” Ben said.

“Confirmed.”

“Run the secondary system.”

“Already matching.”

The child’s right hand curled slightly.

Not a spasm.

Her fingers closed, held, then released.

“Twenty-four percent.”

The room had changed.

Nobody was tired now.

“Twenty-seven.”

Ben felt something dangerous move inside him.

Not belief.

Something smaller.

The possibility of belief.

“Thirty-one percent.”

Venn stepped away from his chemical controls.

“That can’t be right.”

“Both systems agree,” the technician said.

“Thirty-four.”

Ben moved closer to the observation glass.

The child’s face remained still.

Her hair drifted.

Her heart beat.

“Thirty-seven.”

Emily whispered, “Come on.”

Ben heard her.

He pretended he hadn’t.

“Forty percent.”

The lights flickered.

It was so slight Ben initially thought he had blinked.

The low hum of the cooling system dipped.

Every monitor went black.

“No!”

Someone gasped.

Then the room returned.

Displays flashed through recovery screens. Emergency lighting remained active for less than a second before the primary grid stabilized.

“What happened?” Ben said.

“Power fluctuation,” Venn answered.

“How long?”

“Checking.”

The chamber remained illuminated. The child floated undisturbed.

"Primary voltage surged, dropped, then normalized," someone said.

Ben frowned.

"That’s never happened before," one of the engineers muttered.

“Log everything,” Ben said.

“Already doing it.”

“Backup never fully engaged.”

“Duration?”

“Zero point six eight seconds.”

Ben looked toward the coherence display.

The number was gone.

“Restore neurological monitoring.”

“System is rebooting.”

“Chemical control?”

Venn stared at his console.

“I’m checking.”

Warnings began appearing across the screen.

Red.

Then more red.

Ben crossed the room.

“What happened?”

“The injectors lost synchronization.”

“How many?”

Venn did not answer.

“How many, Elias?”

“All of them.”

For one second, nobody moved.

Ben looked through the glass.

Subject 247’s heart continued to beat.

“Stop the sequence.”

“I can’t.”

“What do you mean, you can’t?”

“The reservoirs discharged during the interruption.”

Ben turned.

“All fifty-four?”

Venn expanded the display.

Every reservoir showed empty.

“How much entered her?”

“I don’t know.”

“Retrieve the flow logs.”

“They’re corrupted.”

“The injectors record locally.”

“They should.”

“Then pull the local data.”

Venn’s hands moved across the controls.

Nothing appeared.

“No records.”

“Residual volume?”

“Zero.”

Fifty-four compounds.

Unknown quantities.

Unknown timing.

An entire neurological sequence collapsed into less than a second.

Ben reached for the emergency cutoff.

“If full volumes entered at once, her heart should have stopped.”

“It didn’t,” Emily said.

“I can see that.”

“Her blood pressure is rising.”

The neurological display returned.

Everyone looked at it.

The technician stared.

“What?” Ben asked.

No answer.

“What is it?”

The technician swallowed.

“Forty-eight percent.”

The room went silent.

Ben looked at the screen.

The pattern was no longer scattered.

Activity moved through the brain in coordinated waves.

“Recalibrate,” Venn said.

“It already did.”

“Run it again.”

“Fifty-two percent.”

Emily moved closer.

“It’s still climbing.”

“Fifty-six.”

The child’s heart rate accelerated.

“Temperature rising,” someone said.

“Begin cooling,” Ben ordered.

“Already compensating.”

“Sixty-one percent.”

The chamber alarm sounded.

Ben’s hand hovered over the drain control.

The integration sequence was ruined.

Every protocol said to stop.

Every instinct told him something had already begun that no protocol understood.

“Sixty-five percent.”

The child’s fingers moved again.

Her hand closed around nothing.

Held.

Released.

“Sixty-nine.”

Emily forgot to breathe.

Ben realized he had too.

“Seventy-two percent.”

That was within the lower range of sedation.

Within the range of a human mind temporarily pushed beneath awareness.

Not an empty brain.

A sleeping one.

“Drain the chamber,” Ben said.

Venn looked at him. “The integration isn’t complete.”

“The integration no longer exists. Drain it.”

“We don’t know what a pressure shift will do.”

“We don’t know what any of this is doing.”

Ben entered the manual release code.

Pumps engaged.

The clear fluid began to lower.

The child descended with it until her bare feet touched the chamber floor. Her knees flexed beneath her. The white integration garment clung close as the fluid dropped past her shoulders.

“Respiratory team ready,” Ben said.

Technicians moved toward the chamber.

“Seventy-six percent.”

Her hair settled against her face and neck.

The fluid passed beneath her chin.

Her mouth opened.

She coughed.

A deep, wrenching sound.

Air entered lungs that had never breathed outside the chamber.

The seal released.

Warm vapor rolled across the floor.

Ben reached her first.

She folded against him, wet and frighteningly light. He caught her beneath the arms and lowered her onto the waiting bed.

Emily pulled a heated blanket around her immediately, tucking it over the white garment.

“Airway clear,” Ben said.

“Spontaneous respiration.”

“Pulse one eighteen.”

“Seventy-nine percent coherence.”

Ben pressed two fingers against the child’s neck although the monitor already displayed her heartbeat.

Her pulse moved beneath his touch.

Warm.

Fast.

Real.

She coughed again.

“Pupillary response,” Ben said.

Emily lifted one eyelid and passed a light across it.

“Responsive.”

“Both?”

“Yes.”

“Pain response?”

Emily hesitated.

“Check it.”

She pinched the skin of the child’s forearm.

The arm pulled away.

The child frowned.

Emily froze.

Nobody spoke.

The tiny frown lingered for barely a second before disappearing.

"Did you see that?" Emily whispered.

Ben had.

The expression vanished, but something fundamental had changed inside the room.

That had not been a reflex.

She had objected.

“Coherence?” Ben asked.

“Eighty-three percent.”

The child breathed again.

Her face tightened as if the air itself offended her.

“Normal waking range for a child?” Venn asked.

“Approximately eighty-five and above,” the technician said.

Nobody looked at him.

“Eighty-four.”

The child’s fingers closed around the blanket.

“Eighty-five.”

Nobody moved.

Ben stared at the little hand gripping the fabric.

The child’s eyelids fluttered.

“Eighty-seven percent.”

Her eyes opened.

Brown eyes stared upward at the lights.

Ben stopped breathing.

They moved slowly across the ceiling, unfocused at first.

Then toward Emily.

The technicians.

The chamber.

Finally, they settled on Ben.

There was no emptiness in them.

No mechanical tracking.

She looked at him because she had chosen to.

Her forehead creased.

Ben had rehearsed this moment in every form except reality.

He had prepared questions.

Can you hear me?

Do you understand language?

Do you know where you are?

Do you remember anything?

He said none of them.

The child swallowed.

Her lips parted.

Her voice was rough, quiet, and unmistakably annoyed.

“I’m hungry.”

Nobody moved.

Ben wasn’t sure he’d heard correctly.

Dr. Venn slowly looked around the laboratory, almost expecting someone else to answer first.

The room remained perfectly still.

After thirteen years.

After billions of dollars.

After countless failures.

The first conscious printed human had spoken.

She wasn’t frightened.

She wasn’t confused.

She wasn’t asking where she was.

She was hungry.

Ben felt himself laugh.

A few startled breaths followed around the room, almost laughter and not quite.

It wasn’t relief.

It wasn’t disbelief.

It was recognition.

Somehow...

the very first thing that made her feel completely human wasn’t her heartbeat.

It was her appetite.
