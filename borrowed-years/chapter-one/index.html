Borrowed Years

Read Chapter One

Chapter One

Miles checked his watch before he went inside.

There was no reason to check it. He was not late. No one was waiting for him. No meeting, no shift, no supervisor with a clipboard and an expression that suggested the entire quarterly future of the company depended on Miles arriving six minutes earlier than scheduled.

Still, he checked.

3:14.

The little silver hands told him nothing useful.

Miles stood on the sidewalk with his thumb hooked under the strap, staring at the watch face as though time might offer an explanation if he looked patient enough. Across the glass storefront, painted letters read: HOBB’S CURIOSITIES & ANTIQUES

Under that, in smaller lettering:

BUY - SELL - TRADE - ODDITIES

The word oddities had been what brought him here.

Leah liked odd things. Old things. Creepy little things. Porcelain birds with chipped beaks. Brass keys that opened nothing. Dusty framed silhouettes of strangers. Once, at a flea market, she had spent twenty-three dollars on a cracked ceramic hand because she said it had "a troubling amount of personality."

She had also measured it first.

Leah carried a six-foot tape measure in her purse because beautiful things still had to fit through doors. The ceramic hand now sat beside her front door holding spare keys, two rubber bands, and a grocery receipt she kept forgetting to throw away. She liked damaged things, but she disliked useless ones. That distinction mattered to her.

Miles had laughed then. He could afford to laugh then.

Now he stood outside a shop full of troubling personality with seven dollars in his wallet, a maxed-out credit card, and a proposal still bruised in the air between them.

She had not said no because she did not love him.

That was the worst part.

If she had stopped loving him, at least the pain would have had a shape. A clean edge. Something he could point to and say, There. That’s where it broke.

Instead, Leah had cried.

"Miles," she had said, holding his hands, not the ring, because there had not really been a ring. "I love you. You know I do."

He had known.

"But this isn’t a plan."

He had hated her a little for being right.

"I want a real ring," she had said. "Not an expensive one. One you chose for me because we were ready, not one you found because you were scared."

Miles had looked down at his grandmother’s band.

"And I want to know what happens Monday," she said.

"I apply for jobs."

"Which jobs?"

He had no answer.

Leah worked for a neighborhood housing organization. She spent her days helping frightened people turn disasters into forms, deposits, bus routes, and keys. She knew plans failed. She also knew that refusing to make one left the consequences for somebody else to carry.

"I want to marry you," she had said. "I don’t want to become the plan you didn’t make."

The "ring" had been his grandmother’s old silver band, too small for Leah, too plain, and polished with the kind of desperation that made it look worse. He had told himself it was romantic. Sentimental. Temporary. A placeholder.

Leah had looked at it and seen what he had tried not to see.

A man with no job asking her to step into a future he could not describe.

So now he was here to buy a peace offering. Not an apology exactly. Not a replacement. Something small and strange enough to say, I know you. I still know you.

Miles let go of the watch strap and pushed open the door.

A bell above him gave a tired jangle.

The shop smelled like dust, lemon oil, old paper, and something faintly metallic. It was narrower than it looked from outside, packed tight with leaning shelves and glass cases and little islands of furniture arranged without any obvious system. Lamps crowded together like conspirators. A taxidermied fox stared down from above a bookcase with an expression of permanent accusation. There were jars of buttons, trays of pocket watches, old postcards, opera glasses, rusted tools, military medals, cracked dolls, and one enormous wooden mask Miles refused to look at for more than half a second.

Behind the counter sat an older man with a paperback folded in one hand and a mug of coffee in the other.

He did not look up.

"Afternoon," the man said.

"Afternoon."

Miles drifted between the aisles, trying to imagine Leah’s face lighting up.

The trouble was, every object seemed to belong either to a dead person or a richer one.

A little brass telescope: $85.

A Victorian mourning brooch: $140.

A silver locket with initials worn nearly smooth: $60.

A painted wooden horse with one missing eye: $45.

He picked up a tiny glass bottle with a handwritten label that said RAINWATER, 1912.

Twelve dollars.

He considered it longer than he should have, then put it back.

Leah might have liked it. Or she might have looked at him with that soft, tired kindness that had become worse than anger.

Miles, you don’t have to buy me things.

Which meant: Please stop trying to solve this with gestures.

He moved deeper into the shop.

The floorboards complained under him. Somewhere, a clock ticked with theatrical importance. Miles found a tray of rings in a glass case near the back and stopped before he meant to.

Most were costume pieces. Big fake stones, tarnished bands, little tags looped around them with string. Even the cheap ones were not cheap enough.

He bent closer.

One small gold ring sat under the glass with a modest diamond at its center.

$2,800.

Miles straightened so quickly his knee bumped the case.

The man behind the counter turned a page.

Miles wandered for another ten minutes, pretending he had not already decided there was nothing here he could buy. He picked things up. Put them down. Checked prices. Did math he already knew. The shop seemed to grow stranger the longer he stayed in it, as if every shelf had been arranged by someone who understood longing but not rent.

Finally, he made his way back toward the front.

"Find anything?" the Shopkeeper asked, still reading.

"Just looking," Miles said.

"That’s what most people buy."

Miles managed a small laugh because it seemed expected.

He stepped outside.

The bell gave its tired jangle behind him.

On the sidewalk, he paused and exhaled. The daylight felt too bright after the shop’s dimness. Traffic moved lazily along the street. A delivery truck hissed at the curb half a block down. Somewhere nearby, somebody was arguing into a phone about cilantro.

Miles rubbed his eyes.

"Lot of weird stuff in there," he muttered.

Then he checked his watch again.

3:39.

Still nowhere to be.

As he lowered his wrist, a flicker caught the corner of his eye.

Not bright. Not dramatic. Just a blink of pale blue-white light, like a fluorescent tube deciding whether it wanted to live.

Miles turned.

A vending machine stood against the brick wall beside the shop.

He frowned.

It had not been there when he went in.

Or it had, and he had walked right past it. That was possible. His mind had been full. He had been thinking about Leah, about money, about whether applying for another position he did not want counted as hope or surrender.

Still.

It was not the kind of thing a person missed.

The machine was old, the big rectangular kind with a scratched glass front and rows of plastic capsules inside. Its metal sides had once been red but had faded into something closer to dried blood. Across the top, in sun-cracked lettering, it said: SOUVENIRS

Beneath that, smaller: TAKE SOMETHING WITH YOU

A young man stood in front of it.

He was maybe twenty-five. Maybe younger. Thin, dark-haired, wearing a gray hoodie despite the warmth. Nothing about him should have been remarkable except his eyes.

Miles noticed the eyes before anything else.

They looked exhausted in a way that did not belong to his face. Not tired from lack of sleep. Not sad from a rough week. Old. Worn down. As if someone had folded decades into him and left the rest untouched.

Miles took a step closer.

"Are you okay, buddy?"

The young man turned.

For a second, Miles had the unpleasant feeling that the man was looking not at him, but through all the versions of him that had not happened yet.

Then the young man said, "It’s broken."

His voice was flat. Not warning exactly. Not invitation. Just a fact too heavy to carry any farther.

Before Miles could answer, the man stepped away from the machine and walked down the sidewalk.

Miles watched him go.

"Odd man," he said under his breath.

The young man did not look back.

Miles turned to the vending machine.

"What do we have here?"

The machine hummed faintly. Its interior light flickered again, illuminating the rows of capsules. Each one was clear plastic, round-topped, like the prize bubbles from old grocery-store machines. Inside them sat ordinary objects, each in its own little slot.

A brass key.

A compass.

A folded postcard.

A pair of blank dog tags.

A child’s crayon drawing displayed flat behind the glass.

A fountain pen.

A tiny snow globe.

A matchbook.

A cracked watch face.

A ring.

Miles leaned closer.

The ring sat in slot B6.

It was displayed upright in its capsule on a little square of dark velvet, absurdly elegant among the cheap plastic bubbles. Gold band. Diamond at the center. Maybe smaller stones along the side, though the glass and curved plastic made it hard to tell.

Below it, a small digital price display glowed: B6 - $10.00

Miles laughed once.

"Oh, look," he said. "A diamond ring."

Then he stopped laughing.

He moved closer until his reflection ghosted over the glass. The ring did not look like costume jewelry. It did not have that too-yellow shine or fake gemstone glare. It caught the weak machine light and returned it cleanly, quietly, like something with nothing to prove.

"Wait," Miles said. "That can’t be real."

The machine hummed.

"Could it?"

He looked around, half expecting the old-eyed young man to be watching from across the street. He was gone.

Miles studied the machine’s front panel. The buttons were square and worn from use: A1, A2, A3. B1, B2, B3. Down to D8. Beside them was a cash slot, a coin return, and a small screen that currently read: MAKE SELECTION

No card reader.

Miles looked where the card reader should have been. Nothing. Just scratched red metal and a sticker peeling at one corner.

CASH ONLY

"Of course," he said.

He patted his wallet even though he knew what was inside.

Seven dollars. Not enough. Of course not enough.

He looked back at the ring.

Ten dollars.

If it was fake, he had wasted ten dollars he should not waste.

If it was real...

Miles swallowed.

If it was real, then for ten dollars he could hold in his hand the thing he had failed to provide. A proper ring. Not a promise made out of apology and family leftovers. Something solid. Something Leah could not look at and mistake for panic.

He went back into the shop.

The bell complained.

The Shopkeeper did not look up.

Miles began, "Do you have an ATM because—"

"Because the machine doesn’t take credit cards?" the Shopkeeper said at exactly the same time.

Miles stopped.

The Shopkeeper turned a page.

For a moment neither of them spoke.

Then the Shopkeeper lifted his coffee mug and pointed with it toward the back corner. "Next to the grandfather clock. It charges three dollars."

Miles stared at him. "You knew what I was going to ask."

"Son, half the people who walk through that door ask me the same thing."

"About the vending machine?"

"About the vending machine."

Miles looked toward the door, then back at him. "What is it?"

The Shopkeeper finally glanced up. He had pale eyes, a graying beard, and the expression of a man who had spent three decades refusing to be impressed.

"It’s a vending machine," he said.

"I mean, is it yours?"

"No."

"Do you know whose it is?"

"No."

"Is that ring real?"

The Shopkeeper’s gaze returned to his book. "People ask that too."

"And?"

"And people usually decide ten dollars is a fair price to find out."

Miles waited for more.

The Shopkeeper gave him none.

The ATM stood beside an enormous grandfather clock whose hands had stopped at 11:11. Miles withdrew twenty dollars and tried not to think about the fee, which felt less like a charge than a personal comment. The machine spat out two tens with a mechanical whine.

When he returned to the counter area, the Shopkeeper said, "If I had a dollar for every time somebody asked me about that damned vending machine, I’d be rich enough to sell this shop and move someplace tropical."

Miles held up one of the bills. "Seems like you’re getting close."

"Dollar thirteen per transaction," the Shopkeeper said. "Retirement is slower than advertised."

"You’ve really had that many people ask?"

The Shopkeeper sipped his coffee. "Hundreds. Maybe more."

"So what is it?"

"I told you."

"A vending machine."

"That’s right."

Miles looked through the front window. From here, the machine was plainly visible beside the shop, glowing faintly in the afternoon.

"Have you ever used it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

The Shopkeeper looked up again, mildly annoyed now. "Because I’ve never seen it."

Miles glanced outside, then back at him.

"It’s right there."

"So people keep telling me."

Miles thought the man was joking. Then he realized he was not.

"You can’t see it?"

"Nope."

"But you know about it."

"I know about rain in Seattle too. Doesn’t mean it’s happening in my shoes."

"That doesn’t make any sense."

"Most things don’t if you stare long enough."

Miles let out a nervous laugh. "And you’re just... fine with that?"

The Shopkeeper shrugged. "First few times, I went outside and looked. Nothing there. After the twentieth time, I stopped wasting the steps. After the hundredth, I installed the ATM."

He raised his mug again.

"I make a dollar thirteen every time somebody pulls cash for a vending machine I’ve never seen. That’s not magic. That’s business."

Miles should have walked away then.

Later, he would understand that.

The strangeness was no longer subtle. It had stepped directly into the room, introduced itself, and asked whether he preferred receipt or no receipt.

But outside, in slot B6, was a ring that looked real.

Ten dollars.

Leah’s face rose in his mind. Not the crying one from the failed proposal. The one from years before, when she had tried on a ridiculous antique hat at an estate sale and bowed like royalty. The one that laughed with her whole body. The one he still wanted to see across a kitchen table ten years from now.

Miles folded the second ten into his wallet and took the first one back outside.

The machine waited.

He stood in front of it. The old-eyed man was gone. The street was ordinary. Cars passed. A woman in sunglasses walked by with a paper bag of groceries and did not look at the machine at all.

Miles fed the ten-dollar bill into the slot.

The machine accepted it with a soft mechanical tug.

The display changed.

PLEASE MAKE YOUR SELECTION

Miles lifted his hand.

There was a second, just one, when he almost pressed the coin return.

Then he thought of Leah looking at his grandmother’s ring and trying to be kind.

"B6," he said.

He pressed the buttons.

The machine hummed.

Something inside shifted, clicked, and began to turn. The capsule holding the ring tipped forward, rolled down a narrow track, and dropped into the retrieval tray with a hollow plastic clatter.

At the same instant, the light behind slot B6 went dim.

Miles bent and lifted the capsule.

It was heavier than he expected.

The plastic bubble had a seam around the middle. He twisted it open.

Inside lay the ring and a folded slip of paper.

The ring dropped into his palm first.

Miles went still.

It was real.

He knew before any jeweler could have told him. He knew by the weight, by the warmth it gathered from his skin, by the way the gold did not feel like a coating over something cheaper. The diamond caught the late afternoon light and fractured it into a small, hard spark.

"Holy hell," Miles whispered.

He almost laughed again, but this time the sound would not come.

The folded paper sat in the bottom half of the capsule.

He picked it up.

The paper was thin and white, printed in tiny black type.

At the top, in bold: EXPERIENCE WARNING #14

Miles frowned.

He read.

This item may result in emotional attachment to persons, places, events, or circumstances not presently existing within your native reality. Persistent grief, longing, nostalgia, and existential confusion have been reported. The manufacturer assumes no responsibility for emotional distress resulting from conclusion of experience.

Below that, in smaller type:

By touching, wearing, holding, activating, or otherwise using this item, purchaser acknowledges full assumption of all emotional, relational, temporal, and existential risk.

Miles stared at the slip.

Then he looked at the ring.

Then back at the slip.

A laugh finally escaped him, small and uncertain.

"Okay," he said. "That’s cute."

He turned the paper over. The back was blank.

Some kind of novelty. Liability humor. A weird shop, a weird machine, a weird little warning to make the whole thing feel like an experience.

Leah would have loved the warning.

That thought hurt.

Miles folded the slip and tucked it into his coat pocket.

The ring sat in his palm, impossible and gleaming.

Behind the glass, slot B6 remained dark. The machine display changed with a faint electric flicker.

OUT OF ORDER

"Yeah," Miles said, barely paying attention. "Sure you are."

He should have put the ring in his pocket.

He should have taken it to a jeweler.

He should have walked back into the shop and demanded answers from a man who had none.

Instead, standing on the sidewalk outside Hobb’s Curiosities & Antiques, with no job, seventeen dollars in his wallet, a three-dollar ATM fee he could not afford, and a future that had recently refused to assemble itself, Miles did what almost anyone would have done.

He tried it on.

The ring slid over his finger as though it had been sized for him.

For one strange second, the world became very quiet.

The traffic thinned to a murmur.

The machine’s hum deepened.

The sunlight on the sidewalk brightened until every crack in the concrete shone like a white line on a map.

Miles looked down at his hand.

The ring was warm now.

Not from his skin.

From within.

He had just enough time to think, That’s not right.

Then a woman’s voice behind him said, "Miles?"

He turned.

The sidewalk was gone.
